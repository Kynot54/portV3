Most of my recent career research focused on the most visible titles in tech, such as AI Engineer. Somewhere along the way, I stopped asking a simpler question: which problems do I actually enjoy working on?

I recently completed a career assessment through the APT platform. I went in skeptical, since most resources I've used have been too generic, outdated, or disconnected from how the industry actually hires. This one gave me more specific direction than I expected. It pointed toward systems-level and cybersecurity work, and that matched something I already knew but hadn't said out loud.

After a few months of reflection, and of questioning whether the results were skewed or an echo chamber of my own thoughts, I kept coming back to the same answer. The problems that hold my attention are how software works at the foundation and how it can fail or be defended. I return to them on my own time because I find them interesting, not because a job asks me to. They're also where my academic background is strongest, and they sit at the core of what I'd teach as an adjunct instructor in Computer Science.

I'm not opposed to modern tooling. I've built a full-stack progressive web app for personal use and use AI tools in my development workflow. The productivity gains are substantial, but so is the responsibility to carefully review AI-generated code for missing requirements and incorrect results. I see that review discipline as an essential engineering skill, and I bring it to every project.

Writing code is also what I enjoy most. I like designing and building software myself, understanding exactly what each line does, and taking pride in the result. Tools help me move faster, but the craft of engineering is what keeps me motivated.

None of this narrows the roles I'm open to. It's simply where my curiosity lives. If you work on low-level systems, infrastructure, or security and enjoy talking about how things work under the hood, I'd welcome the conversation. Longer term, I want to work where AI and security meet.
